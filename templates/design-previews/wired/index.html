The Internet Was Supposed to Make Us Smarter. What Happened?

By Steven Levy · Nov 4, 2026 · 9 min read
Xfin✉

Thirty-three years ago, WIRED's first issue promised that digital technology would set off a revolution in how humans think. We were right about the revolution. We were wrong about which direction it would go.

The early web was a library with the doors propped open. Search engines indexed the world's knowledge and handed it back, ranked by something that loosely resembled merit. Then came the feed, and with it a new kind of ranking—one optimized not for what you needed to know but for what you couldn't stop looking at. Researchers at Stanford now estimate that the average adult reads 40 percent less long-form text than in 2010.

“We built the most powerful learning machine in history and pointed it at our own attention.”

The recommendation trap

Large language models promise to fix this by answering questions directly. But an answer is not the same as understanding, and a growing body of evidence suggests that outsourcing the struggle of finding things out may come at a cost we're only beginning to measure.
